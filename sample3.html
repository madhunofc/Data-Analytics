      2. Methodology report

      2.1 Pressure definition

      Pressure is defined by the match situation alone, never by how a player performed. It combines three layers:

      • Named cricket situations (rule-based). A ball is a candidate pressure ball when at least one rule fires. The rules differ for batters and bowlers, because a moment that is hard for one side is often comfortable for the other.
      • Win-probability gate. Two calibrated gradient-boosted models (1st innings and chase, 5-fold cross-validated by match, Platt-scaled) estimate the batting side's chance of winning. Balls with win probability below 3% or above 97% are removed as dead situations. This removed —% of balls.
      • Leverage (hybrid). The Leverage Index is the expected change in win probability from the next ball, divided by its average. It is one of the rules, so model-detected tension that no simple rule describes is also caught.

      Batting: when is a batter under pressure?

      • Steep chase: required rate ≥ 9 with ≤ 12 overs left
      • Big target (180+) with 3+ wickets down
      • Early collapse: 3 down inside 8 overs, or 5 down inside 15
      • Wickets tumbling: 2+ wickets in the last 2 overs
      • Tight finish: overs 16–20 with win probability 25–75%
      • High leverage: top 10% of balls by Leverage Index

      Bowling: when is a bowler under pressure?

      • Defending a close chase: overs 14–20, win probability 20–80%
      • Under attack: 36+ runs conceded in the last 3 overs
      • Big total building: 1st innings, overs 15–20, ≤ 4 down, run rate ≥ 8.5
      • Breakthrough needed: 36+ legal balls without a wicket, run rate ≥ 8.5
      • Tight finish: overs 16–20 with win probability 25–75%
      • High leverage: top 10% of balls by Leverage Index

      Why it is meaningful. Pressure balls carry about twice the average leverage of normal balls, and play changes measurably under them. Batters lose strike rate and get out more often. Bowlers concede more but take more wickets as batters are forced to take risks. Pressure balls make up —% of balls faced and —% of balls bowled.

      2.2 Performance measurement

      Only pressure balls are scored. Win probability added and match results are not used, so a player is never credited for being on the winning side or blamed when teammates lose.

      • Batters are measured on what the brief calls delivering under pressure: scoring (runs) and survival (not losing a wicket when wickets are already down).
      • Bowlers are measured on wickets and economy (runs conceded).
      • Baseline 1, the league. For every pressure ball, the expected runs, dismissals, runs conceded and wickets come from the league average on pressure balls in the same innings × over × wickets-down band × pace/spin × competition cell, smoothed toward the phase average. A death-over ball is compared with other death-over pressure balls, not with the powerplay.
      • Baseline 2, the player's own normal level. The same calculation on the player's non-pressure balls, with their own expectations, gives "change vs normal".
      • Wicket value. A DLS-style run-expectancy curve fitted to this data converts a wicket into runs. Losing a wicket costs more when several are already down, which is how "wickets down" enters batting pressure.
      Batter, per pressure ball: scoring = runs − expected runs survival = expected dismissals − dismissal Bowler, per pressure ball: wickets = wicket − expected wickets economy = expected runs conceded − runs conceded Run value (for change vs normal) = runs term + wicket cost(over, wickets down) × wicket term Change vs normal = run value per ball on pressure balls − run value per ball on normal balls

      Rating logic

      Each component is turned into a per-ball rate, shrunk for sample size (Section 2.3), and converted to a z-score among eligible players. The final score is a weighted sum:

      2.3 Fair comparisons

      • Data cleaning. The two ball-by-ball files are one table split mid-match (part 2 has no header) and are joined on their shared columns. Super overs, rain-reduced (DLS) matches and no-result matches are removed. Extras runs are largely missing in the source, so each wide, no-ball, bye and leg-bye is counted as one run. Chase targets are made consistent with the recorded winner.
      • Eligibility. Batters need 120+ pressure balls in 12+ matches, and bowlers 150+ pressure legal balls in 12+ matches. This keeps one-off cameos out and still rates — batters and — bowlers.
      • Sample-size shrinkage. Every per-ball rate is shrunk toward the eligible-player average with Empirical-Bayes weights (prior strength estimated by method of moments, bounded to 150–1,000 balls). A player with 150 pressure balls is pulled strongly to the mean, while one with 1,000 keeps most of their own rate. Consistency uses beta-binomial shrinkage.
      • Match context. Expectations are set per innings, over, wickets-down band, pace/spin and competition, so death-over specialists, openers and spinners are each compared with like-for-like situations.
      • IPL and T20I data. Both are used together, because pressure skill should show in either. Each competition type (IPL, T20I, other T20) has its own expected values, so differences in scoring level don't favour one league. Outside the IPL, balls against associate nations and A-teams count 0.6, so runs or wickets against weaker attacks count for less. Knockout matches (finals, qualifiers, eliminators, semi-finals) count 1.25.
      • No outcome bias. Win probability only decides whether a ball is a pressure ball. It never feeds the score.

      2.5 Reliability and limitations

      How consistency across matches was checked

      • Per-match consistency is part of the score: the share of matches with 6+ pressure balls where the player beat the league expectation.
      • Bootstrap rank intervals. Matches were resampled 200 times and the full rating recomputed each time. The charts show each player's 5th–95th percentile rank.
      • Split-half reliability. Matches were split at random into two halves 8 times, scored separately and correlated, then scaled up with Spearman–Brown. Batters: —. Bowlers: —. In the same test in Sample 2, a raw unshrunk pressure-ball average reached only 0.09 and 0.17. Shrinkage and context adjustment make the rating about twice as repeatable.
      • Weight sensitivity. The top 10 was recomputed under four other weightings, including one that scores pressure balls only (no change-vs-normal term).
      • Cross-method check. Two independent methods were also built: a hybrid composite (Sample 1) and an opposition-adjusted ridge model with continuous pressure (Sample 2).

      Where the model is less certain

      —
      • Rule thresholds are judgement calls (required rate 9, target 180, 36 runs in 3 overs). Win probability backs them up but does not tune them.
      • Batting pressure is broad (—% of balls faced), because collapse and big-chase situations stay true for the rest of an innings.
      • Missing extras in the source make targets and required rates approximate by a few runs.
      • Opposition strength is a two-level proxy (full member or IPL vs associate or A-team), not a rating of each attack.
      • Pressure is noisy by nature. Ball outcomes are mostly chance, so the order within ranks 4–10 should be read as a tier rather than an exact sequence.
